Beyond GPU supply chains and model architectures, a residential utility bill is becoming the sharpest bottleneck for frontier AI. When hyperscalers submit duplicate grid interconnection requests across a dozen substations for a single planned campus, that "phantom load" inflates PJM Interconnection capacity auctions years before a single server rack is bolted to concrete.
## An Unsettling Testimony in Ohio

On February 13, 2026, American Electric Power subsidiary AEP Ohio filed [regulatory testimony that jolted the North American energy market](https://www.globaldatacenterhub.com/p/unbuilt-data-centers-electric-bills).
AEP Ohio disclosed to state utility regulators that over the preceding year, the utility had received grid interconnection inquiries from more than 50 customers across over 90 sites, requesting more than 30,000 megawatts (MW) of new power capacity.
To put 30,000 MW in perspective, peak electricity demand across every factory, commercial building, hospital, and household in AEP Ohio's central Ohio service territory runs between [8,000 and 10,500 MW on the hottest summer day](https://www.globaldatacenterhub.com/p/unbuilt-data-centers-electric-bills). On paper, pending AI and cloud computing interconnection requests equal three entire central Ohio power grids stacked on top of the existing system.
The structural catch lies in the ratio of 50 customers across 90 sites. Racing against tight deployment timelines, hyperscalers and third-party data center developers routinely shop multiple jurisdictions at once: a company planning a single campus submits full-load interconnection requests to four or five different substations, intending to build only where permits clear fastest.
Regional grid capacity markets have no mechanism to filter out backup applications. Once a data center interconnection request enters the utility load forecast, regional grid operators must procure enough standby generation two years in advance—and spread that multi-billion-dollar tab across every retail electric meter in the footprint.
## From $28.92 to $329.17 per Megawatt-Day

Across [PJM Interconnection](https://insidelines.pjm.com/pjm-capacity-auction-procures-138318-mw-of-generation-resources-as-work-continues-to-address-growing-electricity-demand/)—North America's largest regional transmission organization, spanning 13 Eastern and Midwestern states plus Washington, D.C., and serving over 65 million people—forecast data center load has already translated into a historic rate shock.
PJM Interconnection relies on a forward capacity market to guarantee grid reliability. Power plants are paid a fixed daily rate per megawatt to remain on standby during extreme winter freezes or summer heatwaves. Once projected load outpaces new power plant construction, capacity clearing prices climb vertically along a steep supply curve.
[PJM Interconnection's official capacity auction records](https://www.globaldatacenterhub.com/p/unbuilt-data-centers-electric-bills) trace the escalation:
* In the 2024/2025 Delivery Year, the capacity clearing price across most of PJM's footprint stood at **$28.92/MW-day**, with total market capacity costs around [$2.2 billion](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/pjm-interconnection-capacity-auction-vistra-constellation/722872/);
* In the 2025/2026 Delivery Year (July 2024 auction), PJM capacity clearing prices jumped more than ninefold to [$269.92/MW-day](https://www.igs.com/energy-resource-center/energy-101/capacity-auction-results--what-it-means-for-your-business-s-electric-bill), pushing the total capacity bill paid by electricity consumers to [$14.7 billion](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/pjm-interconnection-capacity-auction-vistra-constellation/722872/):
* In the 2026/2027 Delivery Year (July 2025 auction), PJM capacity clearing prices hit the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC)-approved price cap at [$329.17/MW-day](https://www.igs.com/energy-resource-center/energy-101/capacity-auction-results--what-it-means-for-your-business-s-electric-bill).
Monitoring Analytics, PJM's independent market monitor, concluded in [its dedicated PJM capacity market assessment](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/data-centers-pjm-capacity-auction-market-monitor/801780/) that existing and forecast data center load growth was the primary driver of the price surge. In the prior capacity auction alone, [data center load accounted for roughly 40% of total PJM capacity cost increases](https://introl.com/blog/pjm-rate-shock-100-billion-data-center-electricity-2026). Even in day-to-day wholesale electricity trading during the first eight months of 2026, [data center load represented 9% of total PJM wholesale power costs](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/data-center-load-pjm-wholesale-market/828917/).
Across Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania, and Ohio, residential households saw monthly electric bills jump 15% to 30% before seeing any direct income gains from large language models.
## Why Federal Regulators Blocked the "Behind-the-Meter" Nuclear Shortcut

Facing five-to-seven-year queues for grid transmission upgrades, cash-rich cloud giants tried to bypass the public grid altogether by co-locating data centers directly next to nuclear power plants for **behind-the-meter power**.
The flagship nuclear co-location deal unfolded in Pennsylvania. Amazon Web Services (AWS) acquired a data center campus adjacent to [Talen Energy's Susquehanna nuclear power station](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/talen-amazon-aws-susquehanna-nuclear-data-centert/750440/) and signed a long-term agreement to draw up to [1.9 gigawatts (GW)](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/talen-amazon-aws-susquehanna-nuclear-data-centert/750440/) of nuclear power from the facility.
From Silicon Valley's perspective, the zero-carbon arrangement looked seamless. Talen Energy locked in a premium long-term buyer above wholesale prices, and AWS secured 24/7 carbon-free baseload power without waiting for public transmission upgrades.
Regulated utilities American Electric Power (AEP) and Exelon immediately filed a formal protest with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) against sequestering public nuclear capacity. The Susquehanna nuclear plant had long served as a foundational baseload generator feeding the public PJM grid. Diverting nearly a gigawatt of existing nuclear output behind the meter for a single private customer creates an immediate baseload hole on the public grid, forcing utilities to dispatch costlier natural gas peaker plants while allowing the co-located data center to sidestep shared transmission maintenance fees.
[The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) rejected the amended Interconnection Service Agreement (ISA) for the Talen-Amazon deal](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ferc-interconnection-isa-talen-amazon-data-center-susquehanna-exelon/731841/), ruling that behind-the-meter nuclear diversion could jeopardize grid reliability and shift transmission costs onto everyday ratepayers. Although Talen Energy and AWS later restructured the arrangement into a [front-of-the-meter grid-connected power purchase agreement](https://www.utilitydive.com/news/talen-amazon-aws-susquehanna-nuclear-data-centert/750440/) while continuing litigation, FERC's ruling ended the illusion that tech giants could buy their way out of shared grid obligations.
## Two Billion Gallons of Water and Cooling Towers in Suburban Backyards

Monthly electric bills are only half the community friction; municipal water supplies and suburban zoning lines form the other.
According to the U.S. Department of Energy's [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) United States Data Center Energy Usage Report](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2024-12/lbnl-2024-united-states-data-center-energy-usage-report_1.pdf), annual electricity consumption by U.S. AI servers climbed from 2 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2017 to [40 TWh in 2023](https://ocp-all.groups.io/g/Sustainability/topic/lbnl_2024_united_states-data/111152191). LBNL modeling projects that by 2028, total U.S. data center power demand will reach [325 to 580 TWh](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/united-states-data-center-energy-2025), consuming 6.7% to 12% of total U.S. electricity generation.
Nearly every watt of electricity fed into a GPU cluster turns into heat that must be dissipated by cooling systems.
In Northern Virginia—the world's densest data center hub, with over 300 operational facilities across Loudoun, Prince William, Fairfax, and Fauquier counties—[public water records compiled by the Environmental and Energy Study Institute (EESI)](https://www.eesi.org/articles/view/data-centers-and-water-consumption) show that Northern Virginia data centers consumed nearly 2 billion gallons of water in 2023, a 63% jump from 2019. In [Loudoun County](https://www.loudoun.gov/6408/Data-Centers-The-Loudoun-Story) alone, roughly 200 data centers drew [around 899 million gallons of potable municipal water in 2023](https://securewater.illinois.edu/data-center-expansion-in-virginia-closing-critical-gaps-for-informed-water-planning-and-permitting/), up roughly 250% over four years.
Local governments long courted data centers as fiscal windfalls that generated hundreds of millions in property and equipment taxes without adding students to public school districts. Over the past two years, however, as individual AI campus footprints scaled from 30 MW to 500 MW or 1 GW, windowless data center halls pushed directly against residential subdivisions—bringing 24-hour low-frequency cooling fan hum, high-voltage transmission corridors, and direct competition with agricultural and municipal water supplies.
According to [Ballotpedia's tracking of data center ballot measures](https://ballotpedia.org/Data_center-related_ballot_measures,_2026), voters and local boards across 13 states—spanning California, Texas, and Midwest and Eastern hubs including Ohio and Michigan—put forward [at least 50 local ballot measures and regulatory actions targeting data center development in 2026](https://ballotpedia.org/Data_center-related_ballot_measures,_2026). Suburban homeowners, farmers, and environmental groups formed a grassroots coalition, pushing dozens of counties to enact zoning moratoria, repeal tax exemptions, or mandate closed-loop cooling and self-funded substations.
## Compute Meets Two Decades of Grid Stagnation

North America's grid fragility accumulated over two decades; AI arrived as the ten-times-faster catalyst that exposed it.
For nearly twenty years prior to the generative AI boom, U.S. electricity demand remained essentially flat. Utilities retired dozens of gigawatts of coal-fired baseload generation while wind, solar, and battery storage projects piled up in fragmented interstate permitting queues. Across regional grids like PJM Interconnection, a new renewable generation project now waits more than five years on average from initial interconnection request to commercial operation.
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's modeling also highlights a wide band of uncertainty, with [its 2028 U.S. data center electricity forecast ranging nearly twofold between 325 TWh and 580 TWh](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/united-states-data-center-energy-2025). If hyperscalers adopt closed-loop liquid cooling at scale, GPU energy efficiency continues to improve, and state utility commissions enforce strict "take-or-pay" collateral requirements that purge speculative duplicate requests from load forecasts, the realized grid shock could land well below worst-case projections.
Regardless of hardware efficiency gains, the old grid cost-allocation contract is over. For a decade, software companies scaled in a digital realm of near-zero marginal cost. As AI enters a heavy industrial infrastructure cycle, every ten thousand GPUs are tethered to copper cables, gas turbines, and a neighbor's electric meter. Who pays for the next hundred gigawatts of grid capacity will define the physical borders of the compute empire.

## FAQ

### Why do unbuilt AI data centers raise electricity bills for residential households?

Regional transmission organizations like PJM Interconnection run forward capacity auctions two to three years in advance based on utility load forecasts. When hyperscalers submit duplicate interconnection requests across multiple substations for a single planned facility ("phantom load"), those speculative requests inflate regional load forecasts and capacity clearing prices. Utilities then pass those standby capacity costs onto all retail electricity ratepayers.
### Why did FERC reject the Amazon and Talen Energy nuclear co-location agreement?

In November 2024, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) rejected an amended Interconnection Service Agreement between Talen Energy's Susquehanna nuclear power station in Pennsylvania and an adjacent Amazon Web Services (AWS) data center. Regulators and utilities (AEP and Exelon) warned that diverting existing public baseload nuclear generation behind the meter for a single private customer reduces public grid reliability, forces utilities to dispatch more expensive gas peaker plants, and shifts shared transmission maintenance costs onto ordinary ratepayers.
### How much electricity will U.S. data centers consume by 2028?

According to the U.S. Department of Energy's Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL), annual U.S. data center electricity consumption is projected to reach between 325 and 580 terawatt-hours (TWh) by 2028—representing 6.7% to 12% of total U.S. electricity generation, driven primarily by AI training and inference clusters.
