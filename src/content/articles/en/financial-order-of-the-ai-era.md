## From the Petrodollar and the Tokendollar to Post-Quantum Bitcoin and the Security Budget of the Machine Economy

**Dr.Jingle | Research | Draft 01**

November 2026

---

It may take less than twenty years for an AI company to operate without a single employee in the traditional sense. Different agents could run its research, production, sales and finances. They would buy computing capacity from data centres, purchase data and services from other agents, pay in digital currencies and set aside some of the proceeds as long-term reserves.

What would such a company’s balance sheet look like? Which currency would it hold? How would it lock in future production costs or preserve wealth it creates today but expects to spend in ten years? And who would bear the cost of keeping that digital wealth secure?

Versions of these questions have already surfaced in research on AI, energy, digital assets and the international monetary system. We seldom consider them together.

On August 25, 2026, the Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) published Benjamin Jensen’s essay *From the Petrodollar to the Tokendollar: Economic Statecraft in the AI Era*. Jensen argues that the United States should use its position in the AI supply chain to build a global market for intelligence services priced in dollars, settled in dollar stablecoins and financed and hedged through US capital markets. [1]

Jensen is proposing policy. His essay poses a question that reaches beyond any policy the United States has adopted. If energy was the indispensable input of the industrial economy, might intelligence become the defining commodity of the next one? What would the institutions built around its trade do to the international monetary order?

I want to take that question a step further. If AI changes what an economy can produce, and blockchains change how independent participants can work together, we have reason to reconsider the relationships among money, capital, property rights and security.

## 1. From the petrodollar to the tokendollar

In 1971 the United States suspended the official convertibility of dollars into gold. As the Bretton Woods system unravelled, the dollar lost an institutional anchor but retained its international monetary role. Over the following decades, international oil trade, US Treasuries, dollar capital markets, cross-border banking and geopolitical relationships helped reinforce that position.

The petrodollar describes an arrangement linking commodity trade, invoicing, investment and international politics. It never required anyone to warehouse oil and issue dollars against the inventory. Oil exporters received dollars and could channel their earnings back into global capital markets through Treasuries, banks and other dollar assets. Trade generated demand for the currency. Financial markets gave exporters somewhere to keep and reinvest their surpluses.

Oil accounts for only part of the dollar’s international standing. The size of the US economy, the depth of its financial markets, the liquidity of Treasuries, legal institutions and the advantages of using a currency that others already accept all matter.

Jensen’s tokendollar proposal extends this logic to AI. Its ingredients include overseas AI infrastructure, dollar pricing for intelligence services, token futures, data-centre financing, insurance and cross-border settlement in dollar stablecoins. From a US perspective, the attraction is clear. Even if the facilities that produce intelligence spread across many countries, the United States could continue to capture financial benefits if technical standards, financing, pricing and settlement remain tied to the dollar system.

There is a difficulty at the heart of the analogy. The oil trade has had decades to define what counts as a deliverable commodity. AI tokens have no comparable standard.

## 2. What is a token worth?

A dollar buys different numbers of tokens from ChatGPT and Claude. Buying the same number from each would still leave open how much useful work the customer receives. Models tokenize information differently, use different inference architectures and consume different computing resources. One might solve a programming task in 10,000 tokens. Another might spend 100,000 and still deliver an incorrect result. Counting output tokens alone would give the second model ten times the apparent productivity.

A token is a unit a model uses to process information and a unit a supplier can bill for. Neither function makes it a natural measure of intelligence. We would learn little about a power station’s output by counting turbine revolutions, or about the value of a transport company by measuring fuel burned. Customers buy metered electricity or a completed delivery. The economic value depends on what that output lets them do.

I would distinguish three measures of intelligence production:

- **Intelligence per Joule** measures useful intelligence produced per unit of energy.
- **Intelligence per Dollar** measures useful intelligence purchased per unit of expenditure.
- **Value per Intelligence** measures economic value created per unit of intelligence applied.

The first two concern efficiency in production and procurement. The third asks how effectively intelligence translates into value in the wider economy. “Useful intelligence” itself has no universal unit. For code, task completion rates, test coverage and observed behaviour provide evidence. Research, invention and strategic decisions are much harder to assess.

Jensen recognises this problem. He argues for metrics based on verified AI work. This may be both the most consequential and the most difficult part of the proposal. Without credible standards for delivery, token futures would struggle to establish a reference price across suppliers. Dollar-denominated bills on several AI platforms could become commonplace while a global commodity market for intelligence remains elusive. Computing capacity offers a more tractable starting point.

## 3. Computing capacity as productive capital

In its 2025 report *Energy and AI*, the International Energy Agency projected that annual global data-centre electricity consumption would more than double to about 945 TWh by 2030, with AI a major driver. [2] A shortage is only one possible outcome. The forecast does, however, bring the physical requirements of intelligence production into focus. It takes electricity, chips, servers and networks to keep a model running.

A supplier can specify the GPU model, memory, hours, cluster size, delivery region, network conditions and service quality. Capacity remains heterogeneous, but a contract can standardize delivery within those terms. A data centre might sell next quarter’s capacity in advance. A model company could lock in part of its future costs, while a lender could use the long-term purchase agreement to underwrite financing for the facility.

Recording, transferring and settling these contractual rights on a blockchain is one form of compute-related real-world asset tokenization, or compute RWA. The holder might own equipment, have a claim to future computing time or be entitled to cash flows under an existing lease. Each claim carries different risks and legal rights.

Time matters here. Yesterday’s unsold server hours cannot be stored like gold and sold tomorrow. Rapid GPU development also exposes a long-term capacity contract to technological depreciation. Power forwards, equipment leases and infrastructure finance offer better precedents for this market than the idea of “compute gold.”

In this arrangement, compute RWA would turn future productive capacity into capital that can be financed today. The tokendollar would supply pricing and settlement for the services that capacity produces. The two could form an asset market and a commodity market for the machine economy. A firm with capacity and steady cash flow would still need somewhere to keep the wealth it accumulates.

## 4. When intelligence gets cheaper, what stays scarce?

For more than a decade, Bitcoin advocates have emphasised its supply ceiling of about 21 million coins. Comparisons have generally been with gold, real estate and sovereign money. AI offers a different point of reference.

If inference costs keep falling and productivity rises sharply in software, research, design and parts of the professions, some capabilities that command a premium today could lose value quickly. Human knowledge would still have value, and physical resources would still be finite. In fields where machines can replicate digital output, however, rising supply and falling production costs could become long-term features.

More efficient computing does not permanently accelerate bitcoin issuance. The mining difficulty adjustment absorbs additional computing power primarily as greater competition among miners. AI uses computation to expand productive capacity. Bitcoin uses it to secure a transaction history under established issuance rules. Both consume energy, but they put it to different economic uses.

The technologies could therefore be complementary. One expands the range of wealth an economy can produce. The other seeks to preserve a form of digital scarcity whose supply does not expand in step with productive capacity.

Scarcity alone does not confer value. Bitcoin’s prospects as a long-term reserve asset for a machine economy depend on continued demand for its monetary properties, credible protection of ownership and the security of its network. Two long-term questions follow, one cryptographic and one economic.

## 5. Bitcoin’s two long-term security liabilities

The first concerns quantum computing. A sufficiently capable fault-tolerant quantum computer could, in principle, compromise Bitcoin’s elliptic-curve digital signatures. Coins whose public keys are already visible face the particular risk of prolonged exposure. When quantum computers might reach the required capability is highly uncertain. A system intended to store wealth for decades or even a century cannot comfortably postpone migration planning until an attack becomes commercially viable.

BIP 360 proposes a new output structure to reduce risks from long-term public-key exposure. BIP 361 discusses migration to post-quantum signatures and the phase-out of legacy signature mechanisms. Both are drafts. BIP 360 alone would not provide a complete post-quantum signature solution. [3][4] Beyond choosing algorithms, migration means deciding how existing assets can move, what happens to old addresses and how original owners can establish their rights.

The second question concerns the long-term security budget. Bitcoin miners earn block subsidies and transaction fees. Under the existing issuance rules the subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks. Assuming approximately 52,560 blocks per year gives the following theoretical issuance rates.

| Approximate period | Subsidy per block | Theoretical annual issuance |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2024–2028 | 3.125 BTC | 164,250 BTC |
| 2028–2032 | 1.5625 BTC | 82,125 BTC |
| 2032–2036 | 0.78125 BTC | 41,063 BTC |
| 2036–2040 | 0.390625 BTC | 20,531 BTC |
| 2040–2044 | 0.1953125 BTC | 10,266 BTC |
| 2044–2048 | 0.09765625 BTC | 5,133 BTC |

The network’s real security budget need not decline at the same rate. Bitcoin’s price and fee income affect miners’ revenue, while mining costs affect what they retain. Still, perpetual price appreciation is an unsatisfactory answer to a security-budget question.

In their 2016 paper *On the Instability of Bitcoin Without the Block Reward*, Carlsten and colleagues showed how a shift from fixed block subsidies to fees could change both the distribution of mining revenue and miners’ strategic behaviour. [5] Budish later studied the economics of permissionless blockchain security, identifying a structural relationship between the recurring cost of maintaining consensus and the one-time gain available to an attacker. [6]

Neither study establishes that a security crisis is inevitable. Both illuminate an expense that cryptography alone cannot cover. Network security is an economic service that must be purchased repeatedly. If more wealth comes to depend on Bitcoin while issuance subsidies shrink, who should pay for its long-term protection? The AI economy may offer one answer.

## 6. Could the machine economy pay for digital property rights?

Traditional finance already charges for asset security. Securities investors pay custody, trading and clearing costs. Firms buy insurance. Banks maintain capital and liquidity. Governments fund courts and other institutions that uphold property rights. Together these are part of the cost of conducting business.

Proof of work provides a different security mechanism with costs of its own. The block subsidy can be understood as a way to finance security in Bitcoin’s early years. Scheduled issuance directs economic value to miners who maintain the network. As the subsidy recedes, Bitcoin will have to rely more heavily on demand for security arising from actual economic activity.

If AI accounts for a substantial share of economic growth, and some participants in the machine economy use bitcoin as reserves, collateral or a final settlement asset, they may have a reason to bear some of the network’s security costs. I think that possibility deserves study.

AI firms with bitcoin reserves, digital asset custodians, compute suppliers and financial institutions could enter into a long-term security procurement agreement. Participants would spend an agreed share of operating cash flow on actual bitcoin settlement, block space, post-quantum migration and other verifiable security services. A voluntary commercial agreement of this kind could operate under existing issuance rules, without a compulsory levy on token transactions.

Consider the orders of magnitude. Suppose annual global AI services turnover reaches $5 trillion. If 20% participates and the parties spend 0.1% of participating turnover on security, the pool would receive about $1 billion per year. At an assumed price of $200,000 per BTC, that is equivalent to 5,000 BTC, about 24% of one year’s theoretical subsidy in the period beginning around 2036. These are hypothetical inputs, not forecasts of market size or price. They show how a small share of a sufficiently large market could fund a meaningful amount of security expenditure.

The size of the pool tells us little about what reaches miners. Buying and holding bitcoin adds no direct mining revenue. Post-quantum research has value, but its costs cannot also be counted in the mining budget. Only the portion that reaches miners, under incentives that support the network, can contribute to proof-of-work security in that way.

There is also a public-goods problem. A firm that pays nothing still benefits from a secure Bitcoin network. Why would it keep paying? The most plausible answer is to connect expenditure to custody, settlement, financing, insurance or risk management that participants need in their own businesses. An appeal for contributions will be hard to sustain unless participants can identify what they are buying.

## 7. Could dormant bitcoin become a future security reserve?

In earlier work on post-quantum Bitcoin, I considered a more radical institutional design. If a quantum computer capable of compromising existing signatures becomes available, some early coins whose public keys have long been exposed could be among the first assets at risk. Their theft would harm the owners and could also undermine market confidence and financial stability.

Could a mechanism prevent quantum theft and eventually place some assets conclusively established as unrecoverable into a long-term network security reserve? One possibility would be to allow ample time for voluntary migration and then establish a process for isolating and recovering particular high-risk assets. Only after stringent property-rights conditions had been met, and with broad consensus on the protocol, would it make sense to investigate transferring any of them into a reserve that paid future miners gradually.

A hypothetical reserve of 500,000 BTC, released evenly over 100 years, would provide 5,000 BTC a year in additional security incentives without raising the original issuance ceiling. This illustrates the mechanism. No inventory of half a million eligible coins has been established.

The hardest obstacle is ownership. Twenty years of inactivity at an address proves neither the owner’s death nor abandonment. An exposed public key gives others no sufficient grounds to redistribute the funds. For some early outputs, cryptography might no longer reliably distinguish the original owner from someone who had recovered the private key using a quantum computer.

A proposal intended to protect Bitcoin could thus defeat its purpose by weakening its ownership rules. Redistributing existing assets would change property rights and effective circulating supply, even if the 21 million ceiling stayed intact. The monetary arrangement would have changed in a material respect.

For now, this thought experiment poses an institutional question. If an old cryptographic proof of ownership loses its force, how can a financial system without a central title registry protect original ownership, monetary scarcity and long-term network security together? My preference is to prioritise post-quantum migration and existing property rights, and to investigate security revenue from external economic activity first. A reserve drawn from existing holdings belongs among tightly constrained, long-term research possibilities.

## 8. Energy markets could reshape bitcoin mining

Electricity connects AI and Bitcoin more directly. Some mining operations have been able to convert cheap local power into a globally traded asset. Where local demand cannot absorb available electricity, mining gives that energy access to a different market.

AI offers another route. The same kilowatt-hour can secure the Bitcoin network or produce an intelligence service. If inference consistently earns more per unit of electricity than mining, regions with reliable power, communications and suitable infrastructure may increasingly favour AI facilities.

The loads have different operating requirements, though. Training and some inference services need relatively steady power and connectivity. Many mining operations have more flexibility to curtail consumption. Future energy infrastructure could serve AI, bitcoin mining, grid balancing and other industrial loads at the same site.

GPUs and mining ASICs have different architectures, capital costs and operating characteristics. The opportunities to share assets lie in electrical capacity, land, some cooling facilities and energy management systems. That suggests another line of research for tokenized real-world assets. Power supply agreements, compute delivery contracts, mining income and grid services could enter a common infrastructure financing arrangement.

Where genuine efficiencies exist, the growth of AI could broaden miners’ access to infrastructure and finance. The commercial test comes down to investment per megawatt, equipment utilization, the opportunity cost of electricity and actual operating cash flow.

## 9. Why a financial order needs several layers

These possibilities suggest a financial architecture for the machine economy in which different instruments serve different purposes. No single new currency has to perform every function.

At the **payments layer**, dollar stablecoins or other regulated digital currencies could handle frequent machine payments, cross-border trade and settlement for AI services. A tokendollar system could develop significant network effects.

At the **capital layer**, energy assets, compute RWA and contracts for intelligence services could form part of productive capital, connecting physical facilities and future capacity to financial markets.

At the **reserve layer**, a Bitcoin network that had successfully migrated to post-quantum security might offer some participants a long-term non-sovereign asset. It could sit alongside gold, sovereign bonds and other holdings used to carry wealth across time and diversify risk.

At the **security layer**, operating revenue from the machine economy could provide continuing, market-based funding for property protection, network security and financial infrastructure.

I use the term *multilayer financial architecture for the machine economy* provisionally. Its central principle is to keep the claims and liabilities of each function distinct. Compute assets depreciate and carry delivery risk. Bitcoin carries market and network risk. Stablecoin issuers assume redemption obligations.

A payment instrument redeemable at short notice can run into serious maturity and liquidity mismatches if its principal reserves are volatile, illiquid or deliverable only far in the future. AI does little to ease that constraint. Automated trading could transmit pressure faster.

Stablecoins should maintain liquid reserves consistent with their regulatory obligations. Compute assets belong in markets that finance productive capital. Bitcoin could serve participants who choose to hold a long-term risk asset or post it as collateral. The contribution of financial innovation here is to establish clear, credible and enforceable transactions among these functions. Putting every asset into a single smart contract would leave the economic obligations unresolved.

## 10. Competition in the machine economy will turn on institutions

In *The Nature of the Firm* (1937), Ronald Coase asked why firms exist when markets can supply production services. His answer centred on transaction costs. [7] When finding suppliers, negotiating, contracting and enforcing agreements is expensive, internal coordination may cost less than buying each service in the market.

AI and blockchains could change that comparison. AI can reduce some of the costs of finding information, carrying out tasks and making commercial decisions. Blockchains could reduce some verification and settlement costs between independent parties. With explicit authority, responsibility and enforcement, activities once practical only inside a large organisation might become viable as market transactions among independent suppliers.

Machine participants will still need credit, contractual obligations, liquidity, ways to manage future costs and credible protection of property. An agent may execute a contract automatically while lacking separate legal personality. It may buy insurance on its own authority, but that power supplies none of the capital needed to absorb a systemic loss.

The US tokendollar proposal would bring AI standards, infrastructure and dollar finance into one network. Other economies have reason to study that possibility, and they have room to make different choices. A country could buy AI services in dollars and develop a domestic compute market. A firm could operate in stablecoins and keep some long-term reserves in bitcoin. A network of machine participants could transact across several currencies and distribute its reliance on credit and security across institutions.

The test is whether participants can enter and leave these systems, convert assets and enforce contracts at reasonable cost. Technical interoperability answers only part of that question. Legal rights, the ability to contain losses, liquidity and the allocation of responsibility matter just as much.

## Closing reflections

In the petrodollar era, the production, trade and financing of energy helped shape international economic institutions. Intelligence may likewise become something produced, traded and consumed at scale.

The monetary outcome could be a more elaborate division of labour. AI expands productive capacity. Compute RWA helps organise and finance productive capital. The tokendollar and other digital currencies could support commercial circulation. Bitcoin might give some participants a non-sovereign way to store wealth over time. To serve as a long-term reserve in such an order, however, Bitcoin would have to address both the protection of ownership and the financing of security.

What increasingly interests me is whether the additional economic activity made possible by AI can sustain a security budget for digital property systems, Bitcoin among them. The direction of bitcoin’s price is a much narrower question. If AI-generated income can pay for verifiable security services, the two technologies would share an economic relationship extending beyond electricity consumption or their appearance in the same investor’s portfolio. Production would generate the revenue, and a portion could flow back to the institutions that protect the resulting wealth.

A stable machine economy would still face three old questions. How is wealth created? How is ownership protected? Who pays for that protection? AI is changing the answer to the first. The other two still call for institutions we have yet to design.
